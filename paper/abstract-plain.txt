Tests of out-of-sample return predictability dispose of the asset's drift by estimating it, and that is where they break. A model with an unpenalised intercept does not reduce to the zero forecast when its slopes vanish, so racing it against zero tests no drift and no conditional predictability jointly. We show in closed form that the resulting Clark-West statistic converges to the t-statistic of the asset's mean return: at Bitcoin's drift a model with no features at all is called significantly predictive in 43% of samples. We therefore eliminate the drift instead of estimating it. A certificate is a non-negative wealth process - one bettor stakes on the signal, a second hedges every candidate drift - whose infimum over the nuisance is bounded by a martingale under the null. Validity is finite-sample and uniform in time, with no bandwidth, long-run variance, bootstrap, asymptotics or refitting, for arbitrary black-box pipelines. It can therefore be monitored daily with no correction for looking, and e-values average under arbitrary dependence, so a research grid needs no joint bootstrap and an overlapping h-step forecast combines into exactly the staggered h-vintage portfolio. The construction closes the sample-size question - the median time to certify an annualised information ratio IR is 2ln(1/alpha)/IR^2 years, about 6/IR^2 at 5% - and we are equally explicit about the price, which the e-value literature does not report for this comparison: matched on level and power the guarantee costs about twice the sample of a fixed-sample test, and against a correctly sized Clark-West we measure power of 0.48 against 0.85 at IR=2. As a positive control the same instrument certifies volatility persistence on the same assets within 148-416 days. On returns it certifies nothing in six years, and its ceiling bounds the incremental information ratio at 2.6 without separating it from zero.
